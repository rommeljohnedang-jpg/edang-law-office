Where a case starts

Many cases begin with a complaint at a police station or with a complaint-affidavit at the Office of the Prosecutor. If the arrest was made without a warrant, in the situations the Rules of Criminal Procedure allow, the person is brought for inquest. Inquest is a short review by a prosecutor of whether the arrest and the evidence justify filing a case at once. Other complaints go through preliminary investigation. The respondent receives the complaint and may file a counter-affidavit. A prosecutor then decides whether there is probable cause to file an information in court. A finding of probable cause is not a conviction.

If an information is filed, the court may issue a warrant of arrest, or in some cases a summons. The article on a warrant of arrest explains what the document requires and what to do when officers serve it. At arraignment the charge is read and the accused enters a plea. If the plea is not guilty, the case is tried. A judgment can be appealed within the period fixed by the rules. Missing that period is often the end of the remedy.

Throughout, the person has the right to counsel, the right to remain silent, and the presumption of innocence. A statement signed at a station, especially one signed without understanding it, can be offered later. If you have been invited for questioning or served with a subpoena, call before you give a long account or sign a paper you have not read.

Bail

Before conviction, bail is a matter of right for a person in custody, except when the charge is an offense punishable by reclusion perpetua or life imprisonment and the evidence of guilt is strong. In that exception the court holds a hearing and decides. The 1987 Constitution states the rule. Rule 114 of the Rules of Criminal Procedure sets the procedure, including when bail after conviction is discretionary. The judge fixes the amount and decides whether to grant bail where it is discretionary. The office prepares the application and the papers that support it. It does not say that bail will be granted, that a particular amount will be set, or that the accused will be released on a chosen day.

What to bring, and what to avoid

Bring every paper you have been given: the complaint, the subpoena, the warrant, the information, the inquest resolution, and any receipt for property seized. Write down the dates and the name of the office. Do not discuss the facts in a public post while the case is pending. Do not visit or message the complainant to arrange a payment until you have asked whether that contact is itself prohibited. In some offenses, including those under RA 9262, contact can be a further violation.
